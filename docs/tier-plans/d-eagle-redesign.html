D-Eagle Hub

Problem statement and full redesign spec · September 2026

The problem

People don't forget their commitments because they lack a place to write them down. They forget because the reminder arrived at a moment when they couldn't act on it, and they swiped it away.

The average smartphone user receives around 46 push notifications a day. Sixty-four percent of users will delete an app that sends five or more notifications in a week. Against that wall of noise, a reminder app that simply pings at a set time is indistinguishable from spam — and gets treated as spam.

The deeper failure is that conventional reminder apps treat every task identically. A dentist appointment and "buy milk" fire with the same sound, the same urgency, the same one-line body text. This flattens the user's sense of priority: when everything looks urgent, nothing does, and the habit of dismissing sets in.

Existing reminder apps are storage. They hold the list and ping at the time you chose. Not one of them knows that you personally break evening commitments, or that your "Finance" tasks slip 70% of the time.

Problem statement

Adults who genuinely intend to keep their commitments still break them regularly — not from forgetfulness, but because generic, context-blind reminders are indistinguishable from notification noise and get dismissed reflexively. No widely available reminder app learns an individual's personal pattern of failure and escalates accordingly.

Why this doesn't compete with Google

Google Reminders, Apple Reminders, Todoist, and TickTick all compete on capture and organization — projects, tags, subtasks, collaboration, integrations. Competing there means losing: they have more engineers and deeper OS integration.

D-Eagle Hub competes on a different axis entirely: follow-through. The product question isn't "where do I store this task?" — it's "why do I keep breaking this particular promise, and what can be done about it?"

The six-commitment ceiling already in the app ("Six is the ceiling. Clarity is the goal.") is a genuine differentiator, not a limitation. Every competitor lets you hoard an infinite backlog. Constraining the day is the product.

What survives the redesign

This is the important reassurance: almost none of the logic gets thrown away. The existing codebase separates logic from presentation far better than a typical app, which makes a visual redesign cheap.

Survives untouched

  • lib/intelligence/ — miss-risk prediction, recommendations, memory, feedback. This is the differentiator described above and is already built and tested.
  • lib/engine/ — statistics, optimizer, dependency graph.
  • lib/critical-cascade.ts — the "Don't let me forget" checkpoint state machine and natural-language deadline parsing. Framework-free by design.
  • lib/commitment-parser.ts — natural-language capture, meeting-link extraction and validation, recurrence detection.
  • lib/recurrence.ts, lib/weekly-snapshots.ts, lib/local-data.ts, lib/preferences.ts
  • server/ push pipeline and the GitHub Actions scheduler
  • All 83 passing tests

Gets rewritten

  • app/(tabs)/index.tsx — new layout: countdown hero, day strip, bottom sheet, capture bar
  • app/(tabs)/_layout.tsx — tab bar removed
  • review.tsx, dashboard.tsx, settings.tsx — become sheet contents and a modal, not tab screens
Meeting links: the capture, validation, provider chips, five-minute warning, and openMeeting deep-linking all survive. What changes is placement — the URL field stays collapsed behind "Add a meeting link", and a commitment that has a link shows a small join affordance on its row. The feature isn't cut, it stops occupying the front page.

The redesign, screen by screen

1 · Home — the only screen

One job: answer "what's next and how long have I got", and let me capture something new. Everything else is disclosed on demand.

  • Day strip — seven days, today highlighted. Tap a day to see it. Replaces a month grid, which would be a second screen pretending to be a feature.
  • Countdown hero — live time remaining until the next commitment, its title, and the Eagle risk line if one applies. Two actions: Done, Snooze.
  • Bottom sheet — collapsed, showing a grab handle, the day's count, and a peek of the next two commitments. Dragged up: the full day, then the nightly review. Must show a visible handle and a content peek, because an invisible gesture does not exist to the user.
  • Capture bar — persistent text field plus mic, the single accent-coloured control on the screen. This is the one primary action.

2 · Review — becomes a sheet state, not a tab

The nightly review is the same content and same logic, reached by dragging the sheet to full height (or by the evening notification's deep link). It isn't a place you navigate to; it's the bottom of today.

3 · Signal — moves behind the paywall

You're right that a normal daily user doesn't need it. Charts are a power-user surface: they reward someone with weeks of history and mean nothing on day one — which is exactly why the current build shows 0% everywhere and looks broken to a new user.

New placement: tap the streak/count in the sheet header to open Signal as a modal. Free tier sees a single honest line ("You kept 4 of 6 commitments this week"). Full charts, category breakdown, and export become paid.

4 · Settings — cut to the vital

The current Settings screen carries eleven or more controls. A daily user needs four. Research is direct on this: a preference system is what keeps notification-fatigued users from opting out entirely — 61% keep an app whose notifications follow their settings. So notification control stays; everything else goes.

5 · About — new

Reached from the avatar. Contains: one line on what Eagle is and what it does (the onboarding sentence, permanently available), the build credit — Built by AGU · Bryt Ma Tech UG — version number, replay intro, and a link to reach you. Small, quiet, and the right home for everything that doesn't belong in daily Settings.

Freemium split

Principle: the free tier must be a complete, dignified reminder app. A crippled free tier gets uninstalled before the paywall is ever reached. Paid is for depth, not for basic function.

FREE Unlimited capture, natural-language parsing, the six-a-day ceiling, countdown, local notifications with Done/Snooze, recurring commitments, quiet hours, nightly review, the plain-language weekly line, export/import, offline operation, no account required.

PAID Full Signal charts and history, Eagle voice alerts, lock-screen/full-screen urgent alerts, custom urgent sound, gentle escalation, home-screen widget, next-of-kin escalation.

Note the shape of that: everything free is the app working. Everything paid is the app trying harder. That's a defensible line a user won't resent.

Build order

Suggested as Tier 19, split so each step validates independently:

  • 19a — Settings cut and About screen. Smallest, lowest risk, immediately visible.
  • 19b — Home rebuild: day strip, countdown hero, capture bar.
  • 19c — Bottom sheet, absorbing Review.
  • 19d — Remove the tab bar; wire Signal as a modal.

Each step ends the same way as every tier before it: npm run validate passes, then commit.
